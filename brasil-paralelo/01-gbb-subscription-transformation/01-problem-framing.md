# Problem Framing: The 15+ SKU Problem

## The Strategic Problem

Brasil Paralelo sold seven separate content subscriptions (Patriota, BP Select, Sociedade do Livro, Clube da Música, Escola da Família, Núcleo de Formação, Acesso Total), packaged and priced as 15+ purchasable plans. Seven products, fifteen ways to buy them.

Annual churn was running at 58% (roughly 7% monthly), and the pricing structure was a large part of why.

### Why It Mattered Now

The company had reached a growth inflection point where the subscription model itself had become a revenue ceiling, not a growth accelerant.

**Acquisition friction:** Presenting new subscribers with a complex plan menu at checkout produced confusion and drop-off. Conversion metrics showed decision paralysis at the plan selection step.

**No upsell path:** Plan differences were based on content access categories, not platform capabilities. There was no natural upgrade journey for a member — why would a "Patriota" subscriber become a "BP Select" subscriber? Content categories didn't map to a clear value ladder.

**Perceived unfairness:** Because plans and promotions had accumulated independently, newer members could end up paying less for more, while long-standing members paid more for less. Some plans withheld content that cheaper plans included. This was a direct driver of cancellations: members weren't leaving because they disliked the product, they were leaving because the pricing felt arbitrary.

**Annual-only commitment:** Plans were sold annually with no monthly option, so the only way in was a twelve-month commitment. This blocked anyone unwilling to commit upfront and gave members no way to scale down instead of cancelling.

**Operations complexity:** 15+ SKUs meant 15+ communication templates, 15+ billing configurations, 15+ edge cases in every downstream system. Every change to refund policy, cancellation flow, or trial logic required shipping across 15+ variants.

**Technology differentiation not monetized:** BP had the infrastructure capability to offer multi-screen access, offline playback, and content resolution tiers — but no pricing model to monetize it. These were platform capabilities sitting on the table.

### Who Was Affected

**New subscribers:** The plan selection menu was a friction point. Converting prospects to paid members required them to understand the difference between 15 options with opaque value propositions, then commit to a full year.

**Existing members:** No member had a path to upgrade to a higher-value tier. Retention strategy relied on content expansion, not platform value. Churn analysis showed no upgrade reasons, and cancellation feedback pointed repeatedly at pricing fairness.

**Operations and marketing:** Every product change required shipping across 15 plan variants. Marketing couldn't run coherent tier-based campaigns when the tier structure itself was unclear.

**Product development:** Platform capabilities (downloads, multi-screen, resolution) couldn't be gated by tier because the tier structure was content-based, not technology-based. This meant you couldn't invest in premium features for premium tiers.

---

## The New Model

| Tier | Launch name | Core differentiator |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Good** | Básico | Single screen, standard resolution |
| **Better** | Intermediário | Multi-screen, offline downloads, Full HD |
| **Best** | Acesso Total 4K | All features, 4K, courses |

Every tier carried the full content catalogue. Differentiation came from platform capability, not from withholding content, which is what removed the fairness problem. Monthly billing was introduced alongside annual, with annual discounted to keep it the better deal.

This created a clear value ladder where each tier upgrade had a tangible, demonstrable benefit — the same model Netflix and Spotify use.
